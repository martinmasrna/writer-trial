# The reader

The person a post reaches does not evaluate it. A post is processed in one glance by automatic machinery that evolved long before feeds, below the reader's awareness and outside their control. A post must clear three gates in order:

1. **Register.** The brain deletes whatever it predicted, so only surprise gets through.
2. **Matter.** An attention triage admits only a few ancient survival categories, with other people at the top; the one other route to attention is a specific gap in what the reader already knows.
3. **Earn the act.** Visible engagement happens only when the act pays the reader in identity evidence and the body is aroused enough to move.

The glance itself happens inside a trance produced by the feed's reward schedule, which removes both top-down supervision and the reason to stop.

## Gate 1: only surprise registers

Sensory neurons mostly encode change, not state. A pressure receptor fires when pressure changes, then falls silent while the pressure continues, which is why you stop feeling your clothes. The same policy repeats at every level: the brain continuously predicts its own input, heavily discounts whatever matches the prediction, and processes mainly the errors. Two consequences follow:

1. **Predicted input is functionally invisible.** It is filtered out before it reaches consideration rather than weighed and rejected. A sentence the reader could have completed themselves registers as nothing, whatever its quality.
2. **Habituation is lawlike.** Any repeated stimulus migrates from signal to background and is deleted like background, at every timescale: a sound over seconds, a phrase over weeks, a format over months. Whatever pattern currently works on a feed is being repeated because it works, and is therefore becoming invisible. Format decay is this adaptation operating on schedule, not audiences consciously tiring.

The reward system enforces the same policy through separate machinery. Dopamine signals **reward prediction error** — the difference between the reward received and the reward predicted — so a fully predicted reward produces no dopamine response at all, not a reduced one. Content the reader saw coming therefore generates no motivational pull no matter how good it is. Surprise is a requirement built into both perception and motivation at the level of the tissue, not a content-strategy preference.

Pure novelty fails for a symmetrical reason: an error the brain cannot parse cannot update its model, so illegible input is discarded as noise. Familiarity is meanwhile actively pleasant — repeated exposure increases liking of a stimulus, because fluent processing feels good. What wins is surprise delivered through familiar, fluent structure. Industrial design named the ratio **MAYA**: most advanced yet acceptable.

## Gate 2: which surprises matter

Surprise is necessary but not sufficient: the world contains infinite prediction errors and almost all are ignored. A triage layer built by natural selection decides which errors seize attention. Its tuning follows asymmetric cost — an animal that misses a predator dies, while one that startles at a harmless rustle only wastes some cortisol — so the detector is biased toward threat. This produces **negativity bias**, the most reliable bias in human attention: bad is detected faster, weighted more heavily, and remembered longer than good of equal magnitude, so a threat beats an opportunity at the same level of surprise. The bias is the detector's tuning, not a cultural habit.

The admitted categories are ancient and few, the recurring stakes of survival and reproduction:

- sudden onset, movement, and things approaching
- faces (detected in about a tenth of a second, faster than almost any other categorization), eyes, and where eyes point
- ancestral threats, sexual cues, infant features, food
- and, above all the rest, **other people**: humans survived through group membership and exclusion was a slow death sentence, so social information has its own dedicated machinery and its own priority class.

What passes both gates is surprising information in an ancient category. The triage has no category for "interesting in the abstract."

### Status: the master variable of the social category

Within the social category, relative standing organizes almost everything, and the brain tracks it as a survival variable: the Whitehall studies of British civil servants found a smooth mortality gradient by employment grade across an entire hierarchy of people who all had food, shelter, and healthcare.

Humans run status through two routes:

1. **Dominance** — status extracted by fear, shared with other primates; the deference evaporates with the threat.
2. **Prestige** — the human addition. A species that survives by copying skills has to decide whom to copy, so it tracks who is good and pays them deference — chiefly in the form of attention — in exchange for learning access. Because people locate prestige by watching where others' attention goes, visible attention compounds.

Read a feed in those terms: a follow is a nearly pure prestige gesture (voluntary deference to someone judged worth learning from), a follower count is a public prestige ledger, and engagement numbers cause further interest rather than merely measuring it. The platform gave ancient prestige-tracking machinery a numerical readout.

Standing is also read through two further mechanisms: (1) people evaluate themselves against similar others, not absolutes, which is why a peer's success stings where a celebrity's does not — and a feed is that comparison engine with the range restriction removed, an unbounded stream of similar-enough others performing their best moments; (2) self-esteem is a gauge, an internal meter of one's current relational value to others, and likes, replies, follows, and their absence are exactly the class of signal it was built to read. Metrics therefore move the meter itself, not just the reader's information about it.

### Curiosity: the second route through the gate

The triage is not the only way a surprise can seize attention. Curiosity is a separate drive with mechanics like hunger, and it fires on a **specific, delimited gap** between what the reader knows and what they could know, regardless of whether the topic falls in a survival category: "there is much to learn about Rome" produces nothing, while "you've used this daily and never noticed what the second button does" produces an itch — though a button is no ancient category. The appetite is literal: information is a primary reward — monkeys sacrifice juice for advance information about an upcoming reward even when the information changes nothing about the outcome — so wanting-to-know runs on the same hardware as hunger.

Its intensity follows an inverted U over existing knowledge. With no model there is no gap to feel: a novice cannot crave the details of a controversy they have never heard of, while the person who knows eight of ten facts is desperate for the missing two. Maximum curiosity sits just past what a specific audience already knows. Maximal itch and maximal reach therefore trade off: the biggest itches can only exist in the most prepared audiences, which are the smallest.

## Gate 3: what earns the act

Registering and mattering cost the reader nothing; an action — a follow, like, reply, share — is where a post asks for something, and the drivers of that decision are not the ones readers report. The narrating self has no reliable access to its actual causes and confabulates with full confidence: in choice-blindness experiments, people picked which of two faces they found more attractive, were covertly handed the other photo, mostly failed to notice, and then explained in detail why they had chosen the face they did not choose. Read behavior rather than stated reasons, and expect every driver below to operate on people who would sincerely deny it.

**Identity runs from act to self** at least as often as the reverse: when internal signals are ambiguous, which is most of the time, people infer who they are from watching what they do. Actions are therefore purchases of self-evidence, and people pay real costs for them. A share is close to the cheapest identity evidence ever available — one tap, mildly public, instantly witnessed: "I shared the rigorous thing; I am a rigorous person." The micro-commitments compound: after three likes on craftsmanship posts, "craftsman" fits the self-description better than it did three taps ago, with the narrator reporting the causation in reverse. The reader's sub-second, unconscious question about a post is whether tapping it makes them more the person they are trying to become, at an acceptable price.

Who is in that market matters more than that it exists. People committed to an identity they have not yet secured compensate by displaying its symbols; the established need no symbols because their record is their evidence. The sharing population for any identity-flavored content is therefore dominated by the identity's **aspirants**, who run a standing self-evidence deficit that each share partially services: founders mostly don't share founder-wisdom, aspiring founders do. Content pitched to impress the established forfeits the far larger, far more motivated aspirant market, and vice versa; they are different products.

Group memberships are part of the self, and a group's standing is felt as personal standing: groups assigned by coin flip produce instant in-group favoritism. Which self is running depends on context — the professional, parental, and tribal selves are activated by the situation and react differently to the same content. The machinery cuts both ways with equal force. Content that lets a group-self perform its membership, articulating the in-group's view better than members could themselves, recruits the strongest sharing motive in the system. Content read as an attack on a group-self is dismissed however true, because claims about the reader's tribe are processed as moves in a status game rather than propositions to evaluate. More intelligence makes the defense better rather than rarer, because sophistication supplies better tools for defending the identity.

**Arousal, not valence, decides sharing.** Sharing is an action, and arousal — sympathetic activation, the body readied to act — is action-readiness: a mobilized body discharges into the nearest act, which on a feed is the share button. High-arousal emotions (anger, awe, anxiety, amusement) spread whether or not they are pleasant; sadness, however moving, produces approval without action.

Every visible act is also a performance. Impression management is automatic and universal — people carry an imagined audience even when alone, because reputation once determined access to everything — and a feed's audience breaks three ancestral parameters at once:

1. **Invisible**: the reader cannot see who is watching, so they imagine everyone.
2. **Permanent**: ancestral impressions faded; posts are searchable forever.
3. **Collapsed**: colleagues, family, and old friends are flattened into one room, so a joke calibrated for one context is read in all of them.

The only safe performance before such an audience is one acceptable to every segment simultaneously, which presses everything publicly posted toward blandness. And because acting carries identity risk while consuming is private and free, the overwhelming majority of any audience never visibly engages; lurking is the rational response to an asymmetric bet, not apathy. Two consequences:

1. Every engagement metric is a biased sample: not what people value, but what they are willing to be seen valuing in front of everyone they know at once. Content people privately treasure but won't publicly touch is a structural category, invisible in metrics.
2. Private-channel sharing (DMs, group chats) escapes the collapsed audience, which is why it grows while public sharing stagnates and why public metrics undercount it.

When someone does act, the act pays three ways at once: (1) evidence to the self-model, (2) a move in the reputation game, and (3) self-disclosure, which is directly rewarding at the neural level — people sacrifice money for the chance to talk about themselves. Against that stack stands the single cost, identity risk. Every visible number on a feed is the outcome of that bet, taken reader by reader.

The reader also runs person-modeling machinery on the writer. Brains model people better than anything else, and a repeatedly encountered writer gets a slot as a known person: a stable character with traits, a voice, expected behavior. Persona coherence therefore compounds — and a break of character is a large prediction error in the highest-priority category, a known person behaving unpredictably, so it is punished like a betrayal rather than noted like an error.

## The state: scrolling

The gates describe the machinery; the scrolling state describes who is operating it, and the answer is close to no one. **Wanting and liking are different systems**, and a feed trains only one of them: rats whose dopamine signaling is destroyed still show the same facial pleasure expressions to sugar but will no longer work for it. Over repetition the two drift apart — wanting sensitizes while liking habituates — which produces the signature state of every compulsive loop: a person can compulsively check a feed they would honestly report barely enjoying, and both reports are accurate.

The dopamine burst also migrates: once a cue reliably predicts a reward, the burst fires at the cue and the reward itself gets none. The pull therefore concentrates in anticipation — the notification chime, the icon, the act of opening — rather than in the content, which is why checking outlives any satisfaction with what is found.

Uncertainty is a reward channel of its own: sustained dopamine activity peaks when an outcome is maximally unknown, at coin-flip odds rather than near-certainty, so uncertain rewards grip harder than certain ones.

A century of reinforcement data says which reward schedule exploits this best. **Variable ratio** — reward after an unpredictable number of actions — produces the highest response rates and by far the greatest persistence after rewards stop entirely. The reason is the design's cruelest feature: on a fixed schedule the absence of reward is information (something changed, stop), while on a variable schedule a drought is indistinguishable from normal operation, so unpredictability destroys the stopping condition itself. A feed is structurally a slot machine: each swipe is a fraction-of-a-second response, payoffs arrive at unpredictable intervals in unpredictable sizes, and infinite scroll removes the end-of-page cues that bounded every earlier medium. A mostly mediocre feed is the machine working as designed: under a variable schedule the drought is the schedule, and ninety dull items teach nothing about quitting.

Dedicated machine gamblers report that they do not play to win — wins almost interrupt — but to enter and stay in "the zone": a trance of smooth automatic responding in which self-awareness, time perception, and bodily needs recede. Extended scrolling is the same state, and losing hours without noticing is the same time distortion the gamblers report. In it, top-down control has receded, so the ancient triage runs unsupervised — faces, threat, status, arousal, with less moderation than almost anywhere else in the person's life — while the self still shops for evidence of who it is becoming, and the schedule has removed the reason to stop.
