# The reader

The person on the other side of a post is not an evaluator working through candidate items. Forty minutes into a scroll they are maintaining a state: a low-grade trance of swipe-glance-swipe, executive control mostly offline, time perception gone. What runs in its place is old machinery — a prediction engine that deletes everything it saw coming, an ancient threat-and-status triage with the supervisor half-asleep, a self quietly shopping for evidence of who it is trying to become, a wanting system trained onto the act of checking and long since decoupled from enjoyment. A post is a candidate payout presented to that, and it gets one glance, priced in prediction error, before the next pull.

## Only prediction error registers

Sensory systems encode change, not state: a pressure receptor fires when pressure changes, then falls silent while it continues — which is why you don't feel your clothes. The same policy runs at every level: the brain heavily discounts what it predicted and processes mainly the errors. Two consequences carry the whole document.

First, predicted input is functionally invisible. It is not weighed and rejected; it is filtered before it reaches consideration. A sentence the reader could have completed themselves does not register as weak content — it registers as nothing.

Second, habituation is a law, not a trend. Any repeated stimulus migrates from signal to background and gets the background treatment: deletion. This operates at every timescale — a sound over seconds, a phrase over weeks, a format over months — so whatever pattern is currently working on a feed is, by virtue of working and therefore being repeated, becoming invisible. Format decay is not audiences "getting bored"; it is the same adaptation that deletes your shirt.

The reward system enforces the same policy independently. Dopamine signals reward prediction error — the difference between reward received and reward predicted — so a fully predicted reward is neurally silent. Not diminished: silent. Content the reader saw coming produces no motivational response no matter how objectively good it is. Perception filters the predicted; motivation deletes it. "Surprise or nothing" is not a strategy heuristic — it is how both systems are implemented in the tissue.

Pure novelty fails too, though: an error the brain cannot parse cannot update its model, so the illegible is discarded as noise — and repeated exposure genuinely increases liking, because fluent processing feels good (the mere-exposure effect). The resolution is a ratio: surprise delivered through fluent, familiar structure. Industrial design named it MAYA — most advanced yet acceptable.

## The triage: which surprises get through

Prediction error is necessary, not sufficient — the world contains infinite surprises and almost all are ignored. A triage layer, built by natural selection, decides which errors seize the spotlight.

Its tuning is asymmetric cost: an animal that misses a predator dies, one that startles at a harmless rustle wastes some cortisol, so the detector is biased toward firing on threat. Hence negativity bias, the most reliable bias in human attention: bad is detected faster, weighted more heavily, and remembered longer than good of equal magnitude. A threat outcompetes an opportunity at the same level of surprise, and this is tuning, not culture.

The categories are ancient and few — the recurring stakes of survival and reproduction, dangers and opportunities alike, with danger weighted heavier: sudden onset and movement, approach, faces (detected in about a tenth of a second, faster than almost any other categorization), eyes and where they point, ancestral threats, sexual cues, infant features, food. And, dominating everything, other people: humans survived through group membership and exclusion was a slow death sentence, so social information has its own dedicated machinery and its own priority class.

What passes the double filter is surprising information in an ancient category — not "interesting" in the abstract. Surprising, and about threat, change, opportunity, or people.

## Status: the master variable of social attention

Within the social category, relative standing organizes almost everything, and it is tracked as a survival variable, not a vanity: the Whitehall studies found a smooth mortality gradient by employment grade through an entire hierarchy of people who all had food, shelter, and healthcare.

Humans run status through two routes. Dominance — extracted by fear, evaporating with the threat — is shared with other primates. Prestige is the human addition: a species that survives by copying skills has to decide whom to copy, so it tracks who is good and pays them deference — attention above all — in exchange for learning access. And because people locate prestige by watching where others' attention goes, visible attention compounds.

Read a feed in those terms. A follow is the prestige gesture in nearly pure form: voluntary deference to someone judged worth learning from. A follower count is a public prestige ledger. Engagement numbers are not measurements of interest but causes of further interest. The platform is not gaming human nature; it gave ancient prestige-tracking machinery a numerical readout.

People evaluate themselves against similar others, not absolutes — a peer's success stings where a celebrity's doesn't — and a feed is that comparison engine with the range restriction removed: an unbounded stream of similar-enough others performing their best moments. And self-esteem is a gauge, an internal meter of one's current relational value to others; likes, replies, follows, and their absence are exactly the class of signal it was built to read. So metrics are felt, not merely noted: they move the meter itself, not just the reader's information about it.

## Curiosity: an itch with a shape

Curiosity is a drive with mechanics, not a personality trait. It fires only on a specific gap — a delimited slot between what the reader knows and what they could know. "There is much to learn about Rome" produces nothing; "you've used this daily and never noticed what the second button does" produces an itch. The drive is real appetite: information is a primary reward — monkeys sacrifice juice for advance information that changes nothing about the outcome — so wanting-to-know runs on the same hardware as hunger.

Its intensity follows an inverted U over existing knowledge. No model, no gap: a novice cannot crave the details of a controversy they've never heard of, while the person who knows eight of ten facts is desperate for the missing two. Maximum curiosity lives just past what a specific audience already knows — so maximal itch and maximal reach trade off: the biggest itches can only exist in the most prepared audiences, which are the smallest.

## Why they act: identity economics

Discard the reasons people give. The narrating self has no reliable access to actual causes and confabulates with total confidence — people handed the photo of the face they didn't choose will explain in detail why they chose it. Stated preferences are press releases; watch behavior instead, and expect every driver below to operate on people who would sincerely deny it.

Identity runs from act to self at least as often as the reverse: when internal signals are ambiguous — which is most of the time — people infer who they are from watching what they do. Actions are therefore purchases of self-evidence, and people pay real costs for them. A share is close to the cheapest identity evidence ever available: one tap, mildly public, instantly witnessed. "I shared the rigorous thing; I am a rigorous person." The micro-commitments compound — three likes on craftsmanship posts and "craftsman" fits the self-description better than it did three taps ago, with the narrator reporting the causation in reverse. A post is a purchasable piece of self-evidence, and the reader's sub-second, unconscious question is: does tapping this make me more the person I'm trying to be, at an acceptable price?

Who is in that market matters more than that it exists. People committed to an identity they have not yet secured compensate by displaying its symbols; the established don't need symbols — their evidence is their record. So the sharing population for any identity-flavored content is dominated by the identity's aspirants, who run a standing self-evidence deficit that each share partially services. Founders mostly don't share founder-wisdom; aspiring founders do. Content pitched to impress the established forfeits the far larger, far more motivated aspirant market — and vice versa. They are different products.

Group memberships are part of the self, and a group's standing is felt as personal standing — groups assigned by coin flip produce instant in-group favoritism. Which self is running at a given moment depends on context: the professional, parental, and tribal selves are activated by the situation and react differently to the same content. The standing machinery cuts both ways with equal force. Content that lets a group-self perform its membership — articulating the in-group's view better than members could themselves — recruits the strongest sharing motive in the system. Content read as an attack on a group-self will be dismissed however true, and more intelligence makes the defense better, not rarer: claims about the reader's tribe are processed as moves in a status game, not as propositions to evaluate.

Arousal, not valence, decides sharing. Sharing is an action; arousal — sympathetic activation, the body readied to act — is action-readiness, and a mobilized body discharges into the nearest act, which on a feed is the share button. High-arousal emotions (anger, awe, anxiety, amusement) spread regardless of whether they are pleasant; sadness, however moving, does not. Valence determines whether people approve of content; arousal determines whether their bodies do something about it.

Every visible act is also a performance. Impression management is automatic and universal — people carry an imagined audience even when alone, because reputation once determined access to everything — and the feed's audience breaks three ancestral parameters at once: it is invisible (unseen, so imagined as everyone), permanent (searchable forever), and collapsed (colleagues, family, and old friends flattened into one room, so the joke calibrated for one context is read in all of them). The only safe performance before such an audience is one acceptable to every segment simultaneously — a strong structural pressure toward blandness in everything publicly posted. And acting before it carries identity risk while consuming is private and free, so the overwhelming majority of any audience never visibly engaging is not apathy — it is the rational response to an asymmetric bet.

Two consequences. Every engagement metric is a biased sample: not what people value, but what they are willing to be seen valuing in front of everyone they know at once — content people privately treasure but won't publicly touch is a structural category, invisible in metrics. And private-channel sharing (DMs, group chats) escapes the collapsed audience, which is why it grows while public sharing stagnates, and why public metrics undercount it.

When someone does act, the act is triply paid — evidence to the self-model, a move in the reputation game, and self-disclosure, which is directly rewarding at the neural level: people sacrifice money for the chance to talk about themselves. Against that stack stands the single cost, identity risk. Every visible number on a feed is that bet, resolved person by person, post by post.

The reader also runs person-modeling machinery on the writer. Brains model people better than anything else, and a repeatedly encountered writer gets a slot as a known person — a stable character with traits, a voice, expected behavior. Persona coherence therefore compounds. And a break of character is a large prediction error in the highest-priority category — a known person behaving unpredictably — so it is punished like a betrayal rather than noted like an error.

## The state called scrolling

Wanting and liking are different systems, and a feed trains only one of them. Destroy dopamine signaling and rats still like sugar — same pleasure expressions — but will no longer work for it. Over repetition the two drift apart: wanting sensitizes while liking habituates, producing the signature state of every compulsive loop — wanting decoupled from liking. A person can compulsively check a feed they would honestly report barely enjoying, and both reports are accurate.

The dopamine burst also migrates: once a cue reliably predicts a reward, the response fires at the cue and the reward itself gets none. Anticipation captures the pull — the notification chime, the icon, the act of opening — not the content. Checking is where the signal lives, which is why checking outlives any satisfaction with what is found.

Uncertainty is a reward channel of its own: sustained dopamine activity peaks when the outcome is maximally unknown — a coin flip, not a sure thing — and uncertain rewards grip harder than certain ones. The most compulsive artifacts humans build are never generosity machines but uncertainty machines.

A century of reinforcement data says which schedule those machines run. Variable ratio — reward after an unpredictable number of actions — produces the highest response rates and by far the greatest persistence after rewards stop entirely. The reason is the cruelest feature of the design: on a fixed schedule, absence of reward is information — something changed, stop; on a variable schedule, a drought is indistinguishable from normal operation, so it carries no signal to quit. Unpredictability doesn't just intensify behavior; it destroys the stopping condition. A feed is a slot machine with the lever rotated: each swipe a fraction-of-a-second response, payoffs arriving at unpredictable intervals in unpredictable sizes, infinite scroll removing the end-of-page cues that bounded every earlier medium. A mostly mediocre feed is not the machine failing — the drought is the schedule, and ninety dull items teach nothing about quitting.

Dedicated machine gamblers, by their own account, do not play to win — wins are almost an interruption. They play to enter and stay in "the zone": a trance of smooth automatic responding in which self-awareness, time, and bodily needs recede. Extended scrolling is the same state — "how is it 1 a.m." is the same report. In it, top-down control has receded and the ancient triage runs unsupervised — faces, threat, status, arousal, with less moderation than almost anywhere else in the person's life — while the self still shops for evidence of who it is becoming, and the schedule has removed the reason to stop.
