# The reader: three levels down

This document goes deep on three things: why brains attend and want (the organism), why people act in public (the self), and what state a person scrolling a feed is actually in (the mechanism). Everything here exists to answer one question: who is on the other side of the post, and what is actually happening in them when your content appears.

Claims are flagged by how solid they are. "Established" means decades of replication. "Solid" means well-supported, mainstream. "Framework" means a productive way of organizing facts, not itself a proven fact. "Speculative" means an extension I'm making that the literature doesn't directly test. The wizard effect dies the moment a contested claim is passed off as fact, so the flags stay in.

---

## Part 1 — The organism: why brains attend and want

### The brain does not report the world. It reports surprises.

Start with a fact about sensory neurons that most people never learn: they mostly encode *change*, not *state*. A pressure receptor in your skin fires when pressure changes and then adapts — falls silent — while the pressure continues. This is why you don't feel your clothes, the chair, your glasses, the hum of the refrigerator until it stops. Adaptation is everywhere in the nervous system, from single receptors up through cortex. The steady state is deleted before it costs any processing. What gets through is the derivative — the difference between now and a moment ago. (Established.)

One level up, the same logic appears as prediction. The current mainstream framework — predictive processing — models the brain as a machine that continuously generates predictions of its own incoming signals and processes mainly the *errors*: the gaps between what it predicted and what arrived. Perception, in this view, is the brain's best-guess model of the world, corrected by error signals; attention is the process of turning up the volume on error signals that matter. (Framework — very productive, increasingly dominant in neuroscience, but the strong versions are contested. The weak version — the brain heavily discounts the predicted and prioritizes the unpredicted — is safe.)

Two consequences, and they are the foundation of everything else in this document:

First, **predicted input is functionally invisible**. It isn't rejected after consideration; it's filtered before it reaches consideration. A sentence the reader could have completed themselves does not register as content. It registers as nothing. This is not a preference or a taste — it's how the tissue works.

Second, **habituation is a law, not a trend**. Any stimulus, repeated, migrates from "signal" to "background" and gets the background treatment: deletion. This operates at every timescale — a sound over seconds, a phrase over weeks, a format over months. Whatever pattern is currently working on any feed is, by virtue of working and therefore being repeated, in the process of becoming invisible. The decay isn't caused by audiences getting bored in some vague sense; it's caused by the same adaptation that makes you stop feeling your shirt.

### The triage: what's allowed to interrupt

Prediction error is necessary for attention but not sufficient — the world contains infinite surprises and almost all of them are ignored too. There's a triage layer that decides which errors get to seize the spotlight, and it was built by natural selection long before anything resembling a feed.

The logic of the triage is asymmetric cost. An animal that misses a predator dies; an animal that startles at a hundred harmless rustles just wastes some cortisol. When the cost of a miss vastly exceeds the cost of a false alarm, the optimal detector is trigger-happy — biased toward firing. Evolutionary psychiatrists call this the smoke detector principle (Nesse; solid as a design argument). It explains the single most reliable bias in human attention: **negativity bias**. Bad is stronger than good — negative information is detected faster, processed more thoroughly, weighted more heavily, and remembered longer than positive information of equal magnitude (Baumeister; Rozin & Royzman; established). Losses loom larger than gains. One criticism outweighs several compliments. A threat headline outcompetes an opportunity headline at the same level of surprise, and this is not a cultural artifact — it's the tuning of the detector.

The triage categories are old and short. Sudden onset and movement. Looming (things getting bigger — approaching). Faces: newborns hours old track face-like patterns preferentially, and adult brains detect faces in roughly a tenth of a second, faster than almost any other categorization (established). Eyes, and where they're pointing. Certain ancestral threats — there's decent evidence humans acquire fear of snakes and spiders far more readily than fear of objectively deadlier modern hazards like cars and outlets ("prepared learning," Öhman; solid). Sexual cues. Infant features — the large-eyes-round-face configuration that triggers caretaking (Kindchenschema; solid). Food, historically calories and now their imagery.

And — dominating everything for our species — **other people**. Humans are obligately social. For roughly the entire evolutionary history that built these brains, individual survival ran through group membership: food sharing, coalition defense, mate access, protection of offspring all depended on standing with others. Exclusion was a death sentence slow enough to select against everything that caused it. So the triage doesn't just include "other humans" as one category among ten; social information gets its own dedicated machinery — face processing, gaze following, intention attribution, reputation memory — and its own priority class.

The upshot: what gets through the double filter of prediction and triage is *surprising information in an ancient category*. Not "interesting" in the abstract. Surprising, and about threat, opportunity, change, or people.

### Status: the master variable of social attention

Within the social category, one variable organizes almost everything: relative standing.

Status hierarchies predate humans by hundreds of millions of years, and in social species rank has direct, physical consequences. The primate fieldwork (Sapolsky's baboons is the famous body of it) shows rank written into stress physiology — chronically elevated cortisol in the disadvantaged positions, with downstream damage to cardiovascular and immune systems. In humans, the Whitehall studies of British civil servants found a smooth health-and-mortality gradient by employment grade, running through an entire hierarchy of people who all had food, shelter, and healthcare — each step down the ladder, worse outcomes, even after controlling for behavior. (Established. The mechanism is still argued; the gradient itself is one of the most replicated findings in epidemiology.) The point: status is not a vanity metric bolted onto human psychology. It's tracked by systems that treat it as a survival variable, because for most of our history it was one.

Now the crucial distinction, because humans do status in a way most animals don't. Henrich and Gil-White's analysis (solid, with good subsequent evidence) separates two routes:

**Dominance** — status through force and fear. Old, shared with other primates. Deference is extracted; it evaporates the moment the threat does.

**Prestige** — status through skill and knowledge. Essentially human, and it exists because humans are the cultural-learning species. Once a species survives by copying skills from others, a new problem appears: *whom to copy*. The solution nature found: track who's good, and pay them deference — proximity, gifts, alliance, praise — in exchange for learning access. Prestige is freely conferred, not extracted, and it's conferred with **attention**. Watching whom others watch is the cheap way to find out who's worth copying, which makes attention itself the tracking currency of prestige.

Read that against a feed and several things snap into focus. A follow is the prestige gesture in nearly pure form: voluntary deference paid to someone judged worth learning from. A follower count is a public prestige ledger. And because humans locate prestige by watching where *other* people's attention goes, visible attention compounds — engagement signals are not just measurements of interest but *causes* of further interest. This isn't the platform gaming human nature; it's the platform giving ancient prestige-tracking machinery a numerical readout.

Two more pieces complete the status picture. **Social comparison** (Festinger; established): humans evaluate themselves relative to similar others, not against absolutes — which is why a peer's success stings in a way a distant celebrity's doesn't, and why envy is most intense at short range. A feed is a social-comparison engine with the range restriction removed: it serves an unbounded stream of similar-enough others performing their best moments. And **sociometer theory** (Leary; solid): self-esteem is not a free-floating self-appraisal but a *gauge* — an internal meter tracking one's current relational value to others. It moves with acceptance and rejection signals because that's its job. Likes, replies, follows, and their absence are exactly the class of signal the meter was built to read, which is why metrics are *felt* rather than merely noted.

### Emotion and arousal: the mobilization system

Under the evolutionary lens, emotions aren't decorations on cognition — they're superordinate programs (Tooby & Cosmides's framing; framework, widely used). Each emotion is a coordinated mode that reconfigures attention, physiology, memory, and motivation for a fitness-relevant situation type: fear for threats, anger for violated bargains, awe for things larger than one's model, disgust for contamination.

The dimension that matters most for transmission is **arousal** — the degree of sympathetic nervous system activation. Arousal is the body's mobilization state: heart rate up, systems readied, *action potentiated*. High-arousal emotions — anger, anxiety, awe, excitement, amusement — prepare the organism to do something. Low-arousal states — sadness, contentment, mild interest — prepare it to conserve and withdraw.

This gives a mechanistic reading to a well-known empirical result (Berger & Milkman's analysis of most-emailed articles, since extended to social platforms; solid): content evoking high-arousal emotion gets shared more, *regardless of whether the emotion is pleasant*. Anger-inducing and awe-inducing content both spread; sadness-inducing content, however moving, spreads less. Sharing is an action, arousal is action-readiness, and a mobilized body discharges into whatever act is nearest — on a feed, the nearest act is the share button. Valence determines whether people *approve* of content. Arousal determines whether their bodies are in a state to *do something about it*.

### Curiosity: the itch with a shape

Curiosity gets treated as a personality trait. It's better understood as a drive state with mechanics, like hunger.

The core account is Loewenstein's information-gap theory (solid): curiosity fires when a person becomes aware of a *specific* gap between what they know and what they could know. The key word is specific. "There is much to learn about Rome" produces nothing. "You've used this feature daily and never noticed what the second button does" produces an itch. The gap must be delimited — a slot with a shape, obviously fillable — for the drive to engage.

The deeper finding is that the brain treats information as a *primary reward*, not merely a means to rewards. Monkeys will sacrifice actual juice to receive advance information about upcoming juice — information that changes nothing about the outcome — and the same dopamine neurons that signal juice prediction errors signal *information* prediction errors (Bromberg-Martin & Hikosaka; solid). Human imaging work shows curiosity states activating the same striatal circuitry as anticipation of food or money, and curiosity improving memory for whatever arrives while the state is active (Kang et al., Gruber et al.; solid). Wanting-to-know is not a pale cognitive cousin of appetite. It runs on the appetite hardware. This wires Part 1 directly into Part 3.

Curiosity's intensity follows an inverted U over existing knowledge (solid). Near-zero knowledge: no curiosity, because there's no model for the gap to exist *in* — a novice doesn't crave the details of a controversy they've never heard of. Complete knowledge: no curiosity, nothing missing. The peak sits at high-but-incomplete knowledge: the person who knows 8 of the 10 facts is desperate for the missing 2 in a way the novice never is about the missing 10. Curiosity is maximal *at the edge of the existing model* — which means the most curiosity-inducing material for any audience is located just past what that specific audience already knows. Depth of craving requires depth of existing structure. (This also means maximal curiosity and maximal reach trade off against each other: the biggest itches can only exist in the most prepared audiences, which are the smallest.)

One tension worth resolving, because it looks like a contradiction: everything above says novelty wins, yet one of the older established findings in psychology is the **mere exposure effect** (Zajonc; established) — repeated exposure to a stimulus increases liking of it. Familiar things are preferred; processing fluency feels good. So which is it — does the brain want new or familiar? Both, in ratio. Pure novelty is illegible and gets discarded as noise; pure familiarity is predicted and gets discarded as background. The sweet spot is *new content in a processable form* — surprise delivered through fluent, familiar structure. Industrial designers had a name for this decades ago: MAYA, "most advanced yet acceptable." The prediction machinery wants its errors, but wants them decodable.

## Part 2 — The self: why people act

### The self is a model, and the narrator confabulates

Drop the intuition that behind your eyes sits a unified executive that knows its own reasons. The working scientific picture is less flattering and more useful: the brain maintains a *model* of the organism — its states, traits, history, trajectory — because it needs one for the same reason it models everything else: prediction and control. "The self" is that model. And part of the model is a narrator that explains the organism's behavior, to others and to itself.

The narrator's defining property: it produces explanations with total confidence and no reliable access to the actual causes. The famous demonstrations: split-brain patients whose left hemisphere fluently explains actions initiated by the right hemisphere on information the left never saw — inventing reasons on the spot, experienced as simply *knowing* (Gazzaniga's interpreter; the classic cases are solid, though some pop extensions are disputed). You don't need surgical patients to see it. In choice-blindness experiments (Johansson & Hall; solid), people choose which of two faces they find more attractive, are handed the *other* photo by sleight of hand, and — mostly failing to notice the swap — proceed to explain in detail why they chose the face they didn't choose. Nisbett and Wilson's older work found the same pattern across dozens of setups: people confidently report reasons for behavior that demonstrably weren't the causes (established as a phenomenon; the strong claim "no introspective access to anything" is too strong).

Hold onto this: the reasons people give — for what they read, share, buy, believe — are the narrator's press releases, not the causal record. This is why asking audiences what they want underperforms watching what they do, and why the actual drivers of behavior described in this document can operate on people who would sincerely deny every one of them.

### The self is inferred from behavior — which makes behavior an identity purchase

If the self is a model, what's the model's evidence? Substantially: the organism's own observed behavior.

Self-perception theory (Bem; established, with known boundary conditions): when internal signals are weak or ambiguous — which is most of the time for most attitudes — people infer their attitudes and traits *from watching what they do*, exactly as an outside observer would. "I chose it, so I must like it." "I keep reading about this, so I must be the kind of person who's into this." The direction of causation runs from act to identity at least as often as the reverse. Cognitive dissonance (Festinger; established) covers the high-tension version: when behavior conflicts with the self-model, the model bends to fit the behavior, because the behavior already happened and the model is the editable part.

Economists formalized the consequence as *self-signaling* (Bénabou & Tirole, Bodner & Prelec): actions carry diagnostic value about the self, so people act partly to *manufacture evidence about who they are* — for an audience of one. The workout matters beyond its physical effect; it's a signal to the self-model that "I'm disciplined," and people will pay real costs for such signals.

Now price the feed in this currency. Every visible micro-act — follow, like, reply, share — is behavioral evidence entering the self-model's evidence stream, at a cost of one tap. A share is close to the cheapest identity evidence ever available: near-zero effort, mildly public, instantly witnessed. "I shared the rigorous thing; I'm a rigorous person." The classic foot-in-the-door effect (established) shows these micro-commitments compound: small consistent acts shift the self-model, and the shifted model then generates larger consistent acts. A person who has liked three posts about craftsmanship is measurably more likely to accept "craftsman" as a self-description than they were three taps ago — and the narrator will report that the taps happened *because* they're a craftsman.

So the earlier formulation — a post is an identity tool — can now be stated more precisely. A post offers the reader a *purchasable piece of self-evidence*. The reader's question, processed in well under a second and entirely beneath the narrator's awareness, is: *does tapping this make me more the person I'm trying to be, at an acceptable price?*

### The incompleteness engine: who actually shares

Which raises the question the surface analysis misses: *who* is in the market for identity evidence?

Symbolic self-completion theory (Wicklund & Gollwitzer; solid, an old and somewhat under-appreciated line of work): people committed to an identity they have *not yet secured* compensate by displaying the identity's symbols. In the original studies, the *less* accomplished someone was in a domain they cared about, the more they displayed its markers — titles, jargon, gear, association. The established don't need the symbols; their evidence is their record. The aspiring have nothing *but* symbols.

This inverts a naive assumption about audiences. The people most driven to share content about an identity are not the people who *have* the identity — it's the people *reaching* for it. Founders mostly don't share founder-wisdom content; aspiring founders do. Established writers don't share writing-advice threads; people who want to be writers do. The sharing population for any identity-flavored content is dominated by the identity's *aspirants*, because they're the ones running a self-evidence deficit that each share partially services. Content that lets an aspirant *perform* an identity they can't yet demonstrate is servicing one of the strongest demands in the system. (Corollary: content pitched to impress the established typically forfeits the far larger and far more motivated aspirant market — and vice versa. These are different products.)

### The performed self: everyone is on stage, and the stage is wired

Everything so far concerns the private self-model. Public behavior adds a second system on top, and the founding text is still Goffman (framework, and among the most durable in social science): social life as performance. People maintain a *front* — a curated, coherent, audience-appropriate presentation — and expend continuous effort on impression management. This isn't pathology or vanity; it's the price of admission to social life, universal across cultures, and mostly automatic.

The machinery runs even without a real audience. People chronically overestimate how much others notice them — the spotlight effect (Gilovich; solid) — and carry a simulated audience in their heads whose imagined judgments shape behavior when entirely alone. Humans evolved under conditions where being watched had permanent consequences, in groups where reputation — the community's stored model of you — determined access to everything: partners, allies, help in famine, mates. Gossip served as the reputation system's update mechanism; Dunbar's conversation-sampling studies found roughly two-thirds of natural conversation time is social topics — who did what, with whom, and what we think of it (the measurement is solid; his further claim that language *evolved for* this is speculative). Reputation is not a modern anxiety layered onto the psyche. It's one of the central variables the psyche was built to manage.

Now put that machinery on a feed, where three ancestral parameters are broken at once. The audience is **invisible** (you can't see who's watching, so you must imagine everyone). It's **permanent** (ancestral impressions faded; posts are searchable forever). And it's **collapsed** — Marwick and boyd's term *context collapse* (solid, and load-bearing): in normal life, people perform *different selves* to different audiences — one self for colleagues, another for old friends, another for family — and these performances never meet. A feed flattens every audience into a single room. The joke calibrated for one context is read in all contexts. The only safe performance in front of a collapsed audience is one acceptable to *every* segment simultaneously — which is a strong structural pressure toward blandness, and an explanation for one of the most important asymmetries in social platforms:

**The gap between private approval and public action.** Every visible act is a performance before the collapsed, permanent, invisible audience, carrying nonzero identity risk — too edgy for one segment, too basic for another, too earnest, too late, wrong tribe. Consumption is private and risk-free. So the default behavior is consuming without acting — the familiar observation that the overwhelming majority of any audience never visibly engages isn't apathy; it's the rational response to an asymmetric bet. And it means every visible engagement metric is a *biased sample*: not "what people value" but "what people are willing to be *seen* valuing in front of everyone they know at once." Content people privately treasure but won't publicly touch is a structural category, invisible in every metric. (Private-channel sharing — DMs, group chats — escapes the collapsed audience, which is why it keeps growing as public sharing stagnates, and why it's systematically undercounted by anyone reading public metrics.)

### Group selves: identity determines what gets believed

One more layer of the self, with its own machinery: the group layer. Social identity theory (Tajfel & Turner; established): part of the self-model *is* group memberships — profession, tribe, nation, subculture, team — and their standing is experienced as personal standing. The minimal group experiments showed how little it takes: assign strangers to arbitrary groups by coin flip, and favoritism toward the meaningless in-group appears immediately. The machinery doesn't wait for a group to matter; membership itself is sufficient.

Which self is running at a given moment depends on context — different situations activate the professional self, the parental self, the tribal self, each with different values and different reactions to the same content (self-categorization theory; solid). And when the active identity is *threatened*, cognition changes function: identity-protective reasoning (Kunda's motivated reasoning, Kahan's extension; solid) means information menacing the standing of one's group is scrutinized for flaws, while flattering information glides through. Notably, more intelligence and more knowledge often make this *worse* — sophistication provides better tools for defending the identity. People do not process claims about their tribe as propositions to evaluate. They process them as moves in a status game their self has a position in.

For the anchored question, this cuts both ways with equal force: content that lets a group-self perform its membership — affirming the in-group, articulating its view better than members could themselves — recruits the strongest sharing motive in the system. And content read as an attack on a group-self will be dismissed, however true, by machinery that is not malfunctioning but doing precisely what it's for.

### Why acting feels good: the disclosure reward and the reputation game

Last piece of Part 2: the direct payoff. Tamir and Mitchell (solid) had people choose between answering questions about themselves for less money or about other topics for more. People consistently sacrificed money to talk about themselves, and self-disclosure lit the same mesolimbic reward circuitry as food and money. Roughly a third to forty percent of ordinary speech is about the self. Sharing one's views, tastes, and experiences isn't just strategically useful for reputation — it's *directly rewarding at the neural level*, plausibly because broadcasting the self was how reputations got built and alliances got formed for a few hundred thousand years.

So a reply, a quote, a share is triply paid: evidence to the self-model (self-signaling), a move in the reputation game (impression management before the collapsed audience), and a hit of intrinsically rewarding self-disclosure. Against that stack of payoffs stands one cost — identity risk. The entire visible economy of a feed is that bet, resolved person by person, post by post.

And one asymmetry to close the loop between writer and reader: the reader is running person-modeling machinery on *the writer*. Brains are better at modeling people than at modeling anything else, and they model persistently encountered media figures as *known people* — the parasocial relationship (Horton & Wohl; solid as a phenomenon). A repeatedly-encountered writer gets a slot in the reader's social world: a stable character with traits, a voice, expected behavior. This is why persona coherence compounds and why breaks of character are punished like betrayals rather than noted like errors — the reader's machinery registered a *person* acting out of character, and person-model violations are exactly the class of event the social triage flags as maximally important.

## Part 3 — The mechanism: dopamine, schedules, and the state called scrolling

### First, unlearn the pop version of dopamine

The folk story — dopamine is the pleasure chemical, hits of it are why things feel good — is wrong in a way that matters, and correcting it is the key to this whole section.

The decisive work is Berridge and Robinson's dissociation of **wanting** from **liking** (established; one of the more secure results in affective neuroscience). "Liking" — the actual hedonic impact, the pleasure of the thing itself — runs on small opioid and endocannabinoid "hotspots," and is measurable even in rats via stereotyped facial expressions to sweet tastes. "Wanting" — incentive salience, the magnetic pull *toward* the thing, the grip it has on attention and motivation — runs on mesolimbic dopamine. And they come apart cleanly. Destroy dopamine signaling and rats still *like* sugar (same pleasure expressions) but will no longer work for it, won't even cross the cage; the wanting is gone, the liking intact. Amplify dopamine signaling and animals work frantically for rewards without liking them any more than before.

They also *drift apart* over repetition: dopamine responses sensitize while hedonic responses habituate. Which yields the signature state of every compulsive engagement loop, from slot machines to feeds: **wanting that has decoupled from liking**. The pull remains at full strength — grows, even — while the enjoyment shrinks toward zero. A person can compulsively want to check a feed that they would honestly report barely enjoying, and both reports are accurate, because want and enjoy are different systems and only one of them is being trained. Every study asking people to rate their satisfaction after social media use and finding it low or negative, against unbudging usage numbers, is measuring exactly this dissociation.

### What dopamine actually signals: the error, again

So what does the dopamine system compute? The answer, from Schultz's recordings of dopamine neurons (established; foundational): **reward prediction error**. Not reward — the *difference between reward received and reward predicted*.

The pattern is precise. An unexpected reward: burst of firing. A reward that's been reliably predicted by a cue: *no response to the reward at all* — the burst has migrated backward to the *cue*, the earliest reliable predictor. A predicted reward that fails to arrive: firing *dips below baseline* at the expected moment — a negative error, felt as the small ugly deflation of an unmet expectation. This is a textbook teaching signal — the same error term that machine-learning systems use to update value estimates, discovered running in dopamine neurons.

Notice what this means, because it's the neural convergence with Part 1's opening: **a fully predicted reward is neurally silent**. Not diminished — *silent*. The content a reader saw coming produces no phasic dopamine response no matter how objectively good it is, for the same reason the reliably-cued juice produces none. Part 1 said predicted input is filtered from perception; Part 3 says predicted reward is deleted from motivation. Two independent systems, same policy: only the error is worth signaling. "Surprise or nothing" is not a content-strategy heuristic. It is how both perception and reward are implemented in the tissue.

Also notice where the burst went: to the *cue*. Anticipation captures the dopamine response; consumption of a predicted reward gets none. The felt pull concentrates at the predictor — the notification chime, the app icon, the act of opening — not at the content. This is why checking behavior is so much more robust than any satisfaction with what's found: the checking *is* where the signal lives.

### Uncertainty is not a bug in the reward. It is a reward channel of its own.

Fiorillo, Tobler, and Schultz (2003) found a second dopamine signal hiding under the first. Train a monkey on cues predicting reward with different probabilities — 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100% — and beyond the phasic error bursts, a *sustained* dopamine ramp appears between cue and outcome. Its size doesn't track the amount of expected reward. It tracks **uncertainty** — maximal at 50% probability, the point of pure unpredictability, and absent at both certainties. (The finding is solid and replicated; exactly how to interpret the ramp is still argued.)

Sit with that: the dopamine system generates its largest sustained signal not when reward is likely, but when the outcome is *maximally unknown*. A guaranteed payoff produces less sustained dopaminergic activity than a coin flip. Whatever the ramp's ultimate computational role, the behavioral fact it accompanies is old and enormous: uncertain rewards grip animals — and people — harder than certain ones. This is the deep reason the most compulsive artifacts humans build are never generosity machines but *uncertainty machines*, and it's the bridge to the next piece.

### Schedules: a century of data on what makes behavior persist

Skinner's program measured, with brutal precision, how the *scheduling* of rewards shapes behavior — and it's among the most solid bodies of data in psychology (established; a century of replication across species from pigeons to humans).

The taxonomy: rewards can come after a number of actions (ratio) or after elapsed time (interval), and either can be fixed (predictable) or variable (unpredictable). The results are unambiguous. **Variable ratio** — reward after an unpredictable number of actions — produces the highest response rates, the steadiest responding (no pausing after payoffs, because the very next action might pay), and, critically, the greatest **resistance to extinction**: when rewards stop entirely, variable-ratio-trained behavior persists longest, by enormous margins. Pigeons on stretched variable schedules will peck thousands of times per reward and keep pecking through long droughts.

The extinction-resistance logic is worth spelling out, because it contains the cruelest feature of the design. On a fixed schedule, the absence of reward is *informative* — something changed, stop. On a variable schedule, drought is *indistinguishable from normal operation*. A long dry stretch is exactly what the schedule always looked like; it carries no signal to quit. Unpredictability doesn't just intensify the behavior — it *destroys the stopping condition*.

The slot machine is the purest engineered variable-ratio device, and it's the most reliably compulsion-producing artifact humans have built. A feed is structurally the same machine with the lever rotated: each swipe is a response costing a fraction of a second; payoffs — the genuinely interesting item, the status hit, the new reply — arrive at unpredictable intervals and in unpredictable sizes. Two design details complete it. **Infinite scroll** removes the natural stopping cues that bounded every earlier medium (the end of the page, the end of the episode) — its own inventor has publicly said he regrets building it. And a mostly-mediocre feed is not a failure of the machine. Under a variable schedule, *the drought is the schedule*. Ninety uninteresting items don't teach quitting; they're the normal inter-reward interval, and each one deepens the state described below. (Speculative extension worth carrying: gambling research finds *near-misses* — losses that resemble wins — activate reward circuitry and prolong play; the feed's endless supply of almost-interesting content may function the same way.)

### The zone: what state the reader is actually in

The last piece comes from ethnography rather than the lab. Natasha Dow Schüll spent years studying machine gamblers for *Addiction by Design* (solid as fieldwork), and its central discovery breaks the naive model of why people play: dedicated machine gamblers, by their own account, are not playing to win. Wins are almost an interruption. They play to enter and remain in a state they call **the zone** — a trance of smooth, automatic responding in which self-awareness, time perception, and bodily needs recede. The machine's rhythm carries the behavior; the person is, in their own words, gone. The reward has migrated from the outcomes to the *state itself*.

This maps onto extended scrolling with almost no modification, and it forces a revision of what a feed's reader is. The person forty minutes into a scroll is not an evaluator working through candidate items. They are maintaining a state: low-grade trance, diminished self-monitoring, top-down executive control mostly offline, behavior carried by the rhythm of swipe-glance-swipe. Time distortion is the tell — the "how is it 1 a.m." experience is the same report Schüll's gamblers give.

And here the three parts of this document lock together. With executive control receded, what's left running the show is precisely the machinery of Part 1 — the ancient bottom-up triage, unsupervised: faces, threat, status, the arresting and the arousing, operating with *less* top-down moderation than in almost any other context in the person's life. The identity machinery of Part 2 hasn't disappeared — it's what a payout often *is*: the status-relevant item, the self-evidence purchase, the tribe-affirming take. And the whole loop is held together by Part 3's schedule, which has removed the stopping condition.

So assemble the reader as they actually exist at the moment content appears. A prediction engine that deletes everything it saw coming. An ancient triage running with the supervisor half-asleep, tuned to threat, change, people, and rank. A self quietly shopping for evidence of who it's trying to become, performing for a collapsed and permanent audience it can't see. A wanting system trained onto the checking action itself, long since decoupled from enjoyment. Mid-lever-pull, in the zone, half-hoping something stops them.

A post is a candidate payout presented to *that* — and it gets one glance, priced in prediction error, before the next pull.

One closing note, since anyone who understands this document is now on the other side of the machine: a writer on a feed is, structurally, a reinforcement event inside other people's compulsion loops. The same knowledge that explains what stops a scroll also explains what the scroll is doing to the person. ## Epistemic status, consolidated

Rock-solid, bet-the-house: sensory adaptation and habituation; negativity bias; face-detection priority; social comparison; cognitive dissonance and self-perception (with boundary conditions); minimal-group favoritism; wanting/liking dissociation; reward prediction error; reinforcement schedule effects and extinction resistance; mere exposure.

Solid, mainstream, some active debate: prestige/dominance distinction; sociometer theory; information-gap curiosity and information-as-reward; Whitehall gradient (fact solid, mechanism argued); arousal-transmission link; symbolic self-completion; context collapse; identity-protective cognition; self-disclosure reward; parasocial relationships; Fiorillo's uncertainty ramp (finding replicated, interpretation contested); Schüll's zone (rigorous ethnography, not experiment).

Frameworks — organizing lenses, not proven facts: predictive processing (weak version safe, strong version contested); emotions as superordinate programs; Goffman's dramaturgy.

Speculative — my extensions: near-miss dynamics applied to feeds; the specific claim that feed metrics function as a prestige ledger read by ancestral machinery (each component is solid; the composition is an argument).

Deliberately excluded despite being famous: ego depletion, social priming, power posing, and the strong "dopamine detox" framing — all either failed replication or outrun their evidence.
